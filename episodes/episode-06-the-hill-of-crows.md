# Episode 6: The Hill of Crows

**Status:** Complete  
**Word count:** 665 (story only)

## Summary

Climbing the steep eastern scree, Mara and Fen reach the cairn-crowned Hill of Crows overlooking the Stone Hearth. From the ridge, they witness the scarred captain’s true plan—and a startled flock of crows threatens to expose their hiding place before dawn.

## Story

The scree gave way to flat, wind-planed slate near the summit, where five crooked cairns stood like broken teeth against the sky.

Crows nested in every hollow between the stones. They did not caw at Mara and Fen; they only watched with bright, bead-black eyes, shuffling sideways along the rocks as the two crept toward the northern rim.

Below the hill lay a bowl of grey stone—the Stone Hearth.

Mara had expected a ruined keep or a walled gate. Instead, she saw a sunken ring of standing pillars, each carved with the circle and three cuts, enclosing a broad flat slab of dark granite. At the centre of the slab stood an iron tripod taller than a man, its empty bowl crusted with white ash like the brazier in Vale Hollow.

Around the outer ring, twenty horses stood tethered in neat rows. Red-pennant lances leaned against the pillars.

Fen pressed flat beside a cairn, his breath shallow. "They're already here."

"Not all of them," Mara whispered. "Look at the granite slab."

Two riders were dragging heavy iron chains across the stone, bolting them to rings sunk into the base of the tripod. Beside them stood the scarred captain, his pale horse saddled and waiting at the edge of the ring. He held no lance. In his gloved hands was a sealed clay jar wrapped in red cloth.

"They aren't searching for us anymore," Mara said, her throat dry. "They're locking the Hearth."

As if the wind carried her words, the captain turned toward the tripod and spoke to the men beside him. His voice drifted faintly up the slate slope, thin but clear in the cold air.

"—pitch and salt-iron," he said, tapping the clay jar. "Once the bowl is choked, the northern lamps won't answer Vale Hollow again. Let the green banners march on frozen stone."

Fen gripped Mara's arm. "Green banners. Like the ember showed you."

Mara's fingers tightened around her pocket, where Calder's copper token lay beside the wrapped brass needle. Now she understood why the captain had turned pale in the village square. He had not come to Vale Hollow to steal the ember for himself. He had come to stop anyone from carrying its compass north before his men could choke the Stone Hearth forever.

If the captain poured pitch and salt-iron into that tripod, the old road would go dark—not for twenty years, as the watchtower had, but for good.

Beneath Mara's ribs, the brass needle grew suddenly hot. Even wrapped in wool, it pulsed once, sharp as a struck flint.

One of the crows perched atop the nearest cairn gave a harsh, rattling cry and launched itself into the air.

Then ten rose. Then fifty.

The black flock wheeled upward from the summit in a clattering storm of wings, circling directly above Mara and Fen's cairn.

Down in the Stone Hearth, the scarred captain stopped with the clay jar half-raised. He looked up at the swirling birds, his pale scars catching the grey light. He said something sharp to the nearest rider, and three men dropped their chains and reached for their bows.

Fen flattened himself against the slate. "They saw the crows."

"They didn't see us yet," Mara said, though her heart beat hard against the hot needle. Across the bowl, beyond the ring of pillars, a narrow fissure cut down from the eastern crags straight toward the back of the granite slab—unwatched while every eye stayed fixed on the hilltop.

"Stay low," she told Fen. "When the riders climb this slope, we go down the back."

From the foot of the Hill of Crows came the scrape of stirrups and the rattle of leather. Three riders were already dismounting to search the cairns.

Mara drew Calder's copper token from her pocket, feeling its three notches under her thumb. There was no time left to wait for help from the valley, and no other company coming up the ridge.

"Come on," she whispered. "Before the jar opens."
